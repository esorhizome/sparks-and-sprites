The locomotion lexicon — 26 movement styles, A to Z

Meet the mote: a small blue creature with one eye and no keyframes. Every card below is a different way for a thing to move — the maths of procedural animation, one letter of the alphabet at a time, from Arrive to Zigzag. Where the bestiary was buttons, the codex a character, and the grimoire a phrase, the lexicon is a tour of the machinery itself: sines and polar coordinates, springs and damping ratios, steering brains, joint chains, verlet bodies, raycasts — ending with G · Gait, a two-legged walker that spends everything the other twenty-five cards earn. Each style is a living loop (it keeps moving with nobody touching it) plus a press reaction (click a running card to aim it, excite it, or shove it — most cards use where you click). Press ▶ Run all, or wake one card at a time. Under each card, open code loads its full source into the editor at the bottom — every style is one self-contained function, written to be read.

The maths is the cast list, not the obstacle. Five families, five kinds of memory: Clocks & circles compute position straight from the clock (nothing stored, nothing can drift); Slopes & springs remember a velocity and bend it (that's all calculus is here); Brains & steering subtract two vectors and call the difference intention; Chains & joints solve where limbs must be (the Law of Cosines and FABRIK, side by side); Bodies & ground let position-minus-last-position do the physics. Every formula on the cards — v₀ = √(2gh), cos A = (a² + d² − b²)/2ad, force = k/d² — is drawn on the canvas where it acts, so the symbol and the motion arrive together.

The editor — pick a style above

Every style is one function, make(u), returning { frame(dt, t), press(x, y) }. The kit u: ctx, W, H (canvas), GY (the standard ground line), the palette (MOVER blue for the protagonist, TARGET amber for where it wants to be, BONE, GOOD, HOT), and the drawing kit: stage() (night backdrop + graph paper), ground(), dot(), ring(), arrow(x1,y1,x2,y2,c) — vectors drawn honestly — mote(x,y,angle) (the protagonist), label(), plus the little maths drawer: len(x,y), clamp, ease, wrapAngle (fold any angle into −π..π), rand(a,b), TAU. Everything else — every spring constant, threshold, and turn rate — lives in plain sight near the top of each function.

The preview is bigger than the cards, and every style is written relative to W, H, and GY — the same code scales up without edits. Click the preview to trigger the press reaction; most styles aim at your click.

Try tweaking: every style keeps its magic numbers near the top. Set the Damp card's ζ rows to 0.1 and watch it ring like a bell; give Arrive a tiny slow radius and it becomes plain Seek; raise the Gait walker's step threshold and it takes long lazy strides; drop Swarm's separation radius and the flock becomes a mob. The universal anatomy: decide what the thing wants (a target, a height, a heading), measure the gap between wanting and having, and close a fraction of that gap every frame. That one sentence is most of procedural animation.

In Godot: all 26 are ported — five family files in scenes/motion/, paged by family in locomotion.gd (grab the Godot project and press its menu's F). The same maths runs unchanged — GDScript spells Math.atan2 as atan2, gives you lerp_angle for free, and Vector2 makes the vector code shorter than the JS. In Unity and Unreal the concepts land one-to-one on Vector3.SmoothDamp, Quaternion.Slerp, Physics.Raycast / FMath::VInterpTo, FQuat::Slerp, LineTraceSingleByChannel — chapter 14 is the full encyclopaedia entry: the maths in plain words, the ten vocabulary clusters decoded, and every engine's spelling.
